## Overview

Institutional price action analysis focuses on identifying the footprints of large market participants (institutions, smart money) through price and volume behaviour. Unlike traditional technical analysis that relies on indicators, institutional price action reads the raw chart for three core patterns: sideways accumulation, aggressive initiation, and strong rejection.

## Why Does Price Move?

Price moves when there is an imbalance between supply and demand. Institutions have the capital to create these imbalances and leave identifiable footprints. Retail traders, by contrast, lack the capital to move price and must learn to follow the institutional trail.

## Who Moves the Price?

Institutions are the primary force behind significant price moves: banks, hedge funds, pension funds, and large proprietary trading desks. They do not trade like retail — they accumulate positions slowly over time, using the market's liquidity to hide their intentions. Their activity is visible only through the volume and price patterns they leave behind.

## Three Core Institutional Patterns

### Sideways Price Action Area (Accumulation/Distribution)

Price moves sideways in a well-defined range over multiple sessions. Volume is typically elevated as institutions build or distribute positions. The wider the sideways area and the longer it lasts, the more significant the eventual breakout.

- Represents institutional position building
- Wider range = stronger support/resistance zone
- Breakout direction determined by the subsequent price action

### Aggressive Initiation Activity (Trend)

After accumulation, institutions initiate a directional move. The initiation is characterised by:

- Wide-range candles with above-average volume
- Little to no wick in the direction of the move (ease of movement)
- Consecutive closes at the extreme of the range

These candles confirm that institutions are driving price with conviction. The trend that follows is likely to persist.

### Strong Rejection (LVN Creation)

A single candle or narrow cluster where price was strongly rejected and reversed. Created by a large institutional order that absorbs all opposing liquidity. Visible as:

- A long wick on high volume (rejection of price level)
- A narrow body at the opposite end of the wick
- A distinct area in the Volume Profile with thin volume above/below

Once created, these levels act as structural support/resistance for future price action — institutions are likely to defend levels where they placed large orders.

## Price Action Trading Strategies

### Strategy 1: Support Becoming Resistance (and Vice Versa)

When price breaks a strong support or resistance zone, the role flips. A support that was strongly defended becomes resistance on the retest, and vice versa.

1. Identify a strong support/resistance zone (sideways area or strong rejection)
2. Wait for price to break the zone
3. Enter on the retest of the old zone from the new side

### Strategy 2: Open-Drive

The first significant move after a session opens. Institutions often initiate moves early when liquidity is highest. Identify the open-drive by a wide-range breakout candle with high volume within the first 1-2 hours.

### Strategy 3: AB = CD Harmonic

A price retracement pattern where the second leg (C to D) mirrors the first leg (A to B) in both length and time. When confirmed with volume at point D, it provides a high-probability entry.

### Strategy 4: Session Open Levels

The opening price of a session often acts as a reference point. If price stays above the open, look for longs; below, look for shorts. The longer the open holds, the stronger the level becomes.

### Strategy 5: Daily Open Levels

Similar to session open but applied daily. The daily open is a self-fulfilling level — many traders and algorithms reference it.

### Strategy 6: Daily/Weekly High and Low

Institutional algorithms often defend the daily and weekly high/low. Rejection at these levels provides mean-reversion entries. A break with volume signals continuation.

### Failed Auction

A breakout that immediately fails and reverses. In a failed auction, the market tests a level, fails to attract counter-party volume, and snaps back. This is the institutional equivalent of a spring/upthrust: a trap that shakes out late entries.
